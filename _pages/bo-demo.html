Bayesian optimization finds the maximum of an expensive black-box function with as few evaluations as possible. It fits a probabilistic surrogate (here a Gaussian process) to the points evaluated so far. An acquisition function then uses the surrogate's mean and uncertainty to decide where to evaluate next, trading off exploitation (sampling where the surrogate predicts high values) against exploration (sampling where it is uncertain).

Press Next step to let the algorithm choose the next point, or click anywhere on the plot to evaluate the function there yourself.

What to try

  • Exploration vs. exploitation. Switch to UCB and move κ. With κ = 0 the algorithm only follows the GP mean and can get stuck on a local maximum; a large κ sends it to wherever the band is widest.
  • Needle in a haystack. A narrow peak is invisible to a smooth surrogate until a point lands near it, so purely greedy strategies tend to miss it.
  • Length scale. The kernel length scale sets how smooth the GP believes the function is. Too large and the surrogate is overconfident between points; too small and it learns almost nothing from nearby evaluations. Tick “Fit by maximum likelihood” to let the data choose it.
  • Noise. With noisy evaluations the GP no longer passes through the points, and repeated sampling of a good region becomes worthwhile.
  • Thompson sampling. Each step draws one random function from the GP posterior (the orange curve) and queries its maximum, so repeated runs from the same data can choose different points.

The surrogate is a zero-mean Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel, fitted to standardized observations; all computation runs in your browser. For my research on Bayesian optimization, see Research and Publications.
